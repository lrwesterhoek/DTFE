DTFE benchmark · 30 September – 1 October 2026 · every number re-measured after the fixes

PS-DTFE versus CosmoDTFE

The same data through both codes: crossed Zel'dovich waves with an exact analytic answer (up to 7.1 million particles) and an 11.8-million-particle piece of TNG100-3-Dark at z = 0. Every estimate is scored against the exact solution where one exists, and every run is timed with its peak memory.

ours: PS-DTFE / DTFE, C++/CGAL, Metal GPU CosmoDTFE: edbe0e8 (upstream HEAD), Julia 1.12, 10 threads Apple Silicon, 10 cores, 64 GB

Verdict

Best results

ours, by a wide margin

Our phase-space density is within 10% of the exact answer at 99.6% of points; standard DTFE (both codes give the identical field) manages 53%, and 24% inside multi-stream regions. CosmoDTFE has no phase-space density, no mass-weighted mean velocity (its multi-stream velocity is off by 25% of the wave amplitude, ours by 0.5%) and no cell averages: its grids are point samples that lose 13% of the mass on a 256³ grid, where our exact deposit conserves it and matches the exact cell averages to 0.34%.

Most efficient

ours, at every size

At 7.1M particles every task both codes can do is 1.4–3.8× faster in ours, whole task included (reading and tessellating), and standard DTFE needs 5–9 GB against CosmoDTFE's 17 GB. On the TNG piece the phase-space slice is 4.0× faster at under a third of the memory. On a 0.26M-particle box the two codes tie on the standard-DTFE slice and ours leads on everything else (2× on the phase-space slice, 4× on the grids); CosmoDTFE's queries are very fast once built, but only after tuning its search-tree depth by hand: at the default it needs 355 µs per point at 7.1M particles (25 minutes for one 2048² slice).

Features

ours

Everything CosmoDTFE computes, ours computes too (identically, to 10⁻⁸, for standard DTFE and the stream counts), plus phase-space densities, dispersion, per-stream records, mass-conserving and exact cell averages, caustics, cosmic-web labels, GPU deposits and boxes larger than memory. CosmoDTFE's real strength is being a small Julia library you can hold in your head and call directly.

Found in ours

6 issues fixed, 3 speedups

The benchmark exposed a bug in our memory auto-tuner and three real costs: point evaluation held every point's stream records in memory, the exact GPU deposit parallelised badly when cells are much smaller than tetrahedra, and the CPU sampled deposit ran serially inside one partition. Fixing them turned up two errors in non-periodic runs: a region cut from a larger cloud collected mass on its faces, and partitioned runs differed along the partition seams. All six are fixed; the TNG region now holds exactly the mass of the particles inside it, and every number in this report is re-measured after the fixes (before and after at the end). Two further speedups came from asking where a small run's time goes — its vertex densities and its triangulation — and a third from letting the auto-tuner split small sets for speed.

How it was tested

  • Synthetic data with an exact answer. Crossed Zel'dovich waves (tests/generate_ps_test_data.py --crossed-waves, A·k = 1.8, 100 Mpc periodic box) at 64³, 128³ and 192³ particles. Every axis maps independently, so the density, stream count, mass-weighted velocity and their exact cell averages all factorise into 1-D inverse maps, computed to machine precision.
  • Real data. TNG100-3-Dark z = 0: every particle that starts in the central half-box Lagrangian cube (11.8M particles, non-periodic). Compared in the 31 Mpc region [40, 71)³ Mpc, 12 Mpc inside the cube's image, where no particle from outside the cube arrives (checked on the full box).
  • Workloads. A 2048² slice of points (4.2M) and a 256³ grid (16.8M cells). Each code runs its own intended method: CosmoDTFE's PeriodicEstimator(DensityEstimator) and PeriodicPhaseSpaceEstimator with streamNumber; ours the PS-DTFE and DTFE binaries.
  • Fair timing. Ours: the whole process, reading and writing included. CosmoDTFE: build plus evaluation, with Julia's compilation and the file reading excluded (both in its favour). CosmoDTFE's BVH depth was tuned per size (18, 21, 22) on a probe; padding 5%.

What the benchmark found in ours, and the fixes

  • The auto-tuner's stream estimate for non-periodic data. It tiled the output region (--box) instead of the particle cloud and wrapped initial positions into it, estimating 8.1 million streams per point on the TNG piece, and then ran the point evaluation one small partition at a time: 222 s instead of 29 s. It now tiles the cloud's own Lagrangian bounding box; ps_auto_tune_check section G fails on the old code (75,593 streams per point on a synthetic clump) and passes on the new. The TNG runs below no longer need the manual override the first measurement used.
  • Point evaluation memory. Every point's stream records stayed in memory until the end. Each thread now packs its records into 16 MB blocks, and the blocks are grouped by point only when the results are written; the output is byte-identical. 16.8M points at 2.1M particles: 28.2 GB → 8.6 GB (CosmoDTFE 8.8 GB). At 7.1M particles the auto-tuner now spends the saving on fewer, larger partitions (2³ instead of 3³) at the same speed; at the old split the run needs 9.5 GB instead of 12.3 GB.
  • Exact deposit with small cells. One GPU thread per tetrahedron left each buffer waiting on its few giant tetrahedra. Each tetrahedron's cells are now split into blocks of 16 that run on separate threads, in two passes through a per-tetrahedron weight total, so every tetrahedron still deposits exactly its mass. Stopped runs now finish: 32 s at 0.26M particles, 135 s on the TNG region, and the 2.1M run drops from 104 s to 57 s. Grids agree with the old kernel to float rounding (tet-touch counts identical).
  • CPU sampled deposit. Now threaded inside a partition, with private per-thread grids merged in a fixed order; on one thread the output is byte-identical to before. A single-partition box goes from 145 s to 20 s. Large boxes were already parallel across concurrently running partitions, so they gain less (7.1M: 131 s → 121 s; TNG 169 s → 145 s).
  • Mass on the faces of a region. Found while fixing the above: in a non-periodic run whose --box region cuts through a larger cloud, a tetrahedron crossing the region's face gave its whole mass to the part inside. On the TNG region the outermost layer of cells averaged 2.04 (exact deposit) and 1.49 (sampled) against 0.70 inside, and the region held 5.6% and 3.6% more mass than the particles inside it. A tetrahedron now keeps only its inside share: the sampled deposits count its samples beyond the face too, the exact deposit weighs its inside pieces against the whole tetrahedron. The face layer now reads 0.71, the region's mass matches its particles to 0.01%, and on a synthetic clump a region reproduces the same cells of a larger grid to the last digit. Counting the samples beyond the face one by one first cost the TNG region's sampled deposits a third (CPU 142 s → 190 s). They are now counted one sample column at a time: along a column the four face tests are linear in the position, so the samples certainly inside form one run found by a seeded binary search, and only the few within rounding distance of a face are tested individually, which keeps the count exactly the per-sample one. Most of the remaining cost turned out not to be the counting at all (1% of the deposit) but the compiler reloading the tetrahedron's geometry on every in-grid sample test once the counting routine had taken its address; handing it copies removed that. Against the pre-fix code on the same session the CPU deposit is back to 138–142 s for 138–140 s (700 vs 701 CPU-seconds), the GPU deposit within its run-to-run spread (113–137 s, old 117–124 s).
  • Partition seams in non-periodic runs. A non-periodic run split into partitions (the TNG region's auto-tuned run uses 27) differed from a single triangulation along the seams: 0.04% of cells, the density by up to 1% of its peak and the velocity by up to 84%. The cause was the flat slivers that fill the space between a finite cloud's nearly planar faces and its convex hull, joining particles up to a whole face apart: the hull of a face is a global object, so each partition built different slivers. The Lagrangian domain is now the cloud's alpha shape, the tetrahedra with a Lagrangian circumradius of at most three mean spacings (interior ones never exceed 0.91, in jittered lattices and in TNG's initial conditions alike). Those are local, so split and single runs now agree to the rounding of their sums, and the slivers' stretched images no longer put spurious mass across the grid.
  • Small runs: the vertex densities. On a 0.26M-particle slice the work the benchmark measures — evaluating 4.2M points — took 0.2 s of a 3 s run; 1.5 s went into the Delaunay triangulation and 0.6 s into the vertex densities, where every vertex walked its incident cells and recomputed each cell's volume four times. The densities are now summed over the cells, in double: 0.6 s → 0.14 s in PS-DTFE, 0.25 s → 0.02 s in standard DTFE (at 11.8M particles the DTFE slice drops from 57 s to 44 s). A density can move in its last float bit; the stream counts do not.
  • Small runs: the triangulation. CGAL can insert in parallel. It helps PS-DTFE's near-lattice Lagrangian points (1.45 s → 0.5 s on six performance cores — more threads only add lock contention, and a thread on an efficiency core stalls the rest) and hurts standard DTFE's clustered Eulerian points (4.5× slower), so only PS-DTFE has it. It is opt-in (--parallel-triangulation): the parallel insert's vertex and cell order differ from run to run, which moves every floating-point output between two identical runs at the level of rounding, and the query server, the tessellation cache and the test suites rely on bit-identical repeats. With the flag the 0.26M slice takes 1.4 s instead of 2.4 s and the TNG region's GPU grid 110 s instead of 135 s; the tables below use the default.
  • Small runs: let the tuner split for speed. There is a way to parallelise the triangulation that keeps every bit reproducible: split a small set into eight partitions and triangulate each on its own core. The memory-driven auto-tuner never did that for sets that fit, so it now predicts the wall time of one, eight and twenty-seven partitions — the sequential triangulation gains, the deposit and point evaluation already use every core, a GPU deposit serialises whatever the split — and splits only for a clear gain. The 0.26M slice takes 1.4 s instead of 2.4 s and its 16.8M point values 4.2 s instead of 5.3 s; grids stay in one domain. Such a split merges its partitions in a fixed order, so repeated runs agree bit for bit (ordering the merge of every partitioned run was tried and rejected: the TNG region's partitions differ thirty-fold in cost, threads idled behind the slow ones for a 35% loss, and its GPU sums are not reproducible anyway). The same pass fixed the tuner's memory report for single-domain runs, which multiplied one domain's bytes by the thread count and told the GUI a 0.26M-particle run needed 52 GB.
